You wake up at 3am again.

Not because of noise. Not because of hunger.

Because your mind won't stop running the numbers.

How much is in the account? Can I cover the rent? What do I tell them if I can't? How long can I keep this up?

You already know the answers. That's the worst part.

You stare at the ceiling and count debt the way other people count blessings. There's the bank loan. The two personal loans from colleagues you now avoid in the corridor. The mobile money borrowing that started as "just this once." The credit card you told yourself was for emergencies only.

Now every day feels like an emergency.

You used to be decent with money. At least, you thought you were. You had a plan. Then things shifted. The economy shifted. Life shifted. And suddenly the ground disappeared under you — and you've been falling ever since, borrowing to repay, repaying to borrow again, watching the hole get deeper every single month.

Your wife doesn't say it directly. But you can hear it in the silence when she checks her phone at the end of the month. You see it in how she looks at you when the children come home from school talking about fees, clothes, excursions, things that cost money you do not have.

I'm trying. I'm working. I'm doing everything I can. Why isn't it enough?

You've cut back. You've sacrificed meals. You've taken on extra work until your body was begging you to stop. You've borrowed from people who now call with a different kind of voice — a voice that means something has changed between you and them forever.

You've tried everything the internet told you to try. Nothing has worked. And somewhere, quietly, a terrifying thought has started to take root:

What if I never get out of this?

Kuffey A. — sharing the story that changed everything, one reader at a time.

Let me take you back to 2021.

I was thirty-seven. I had a decent job — not glamorous, but steady. My wife Ngozi and I had two kids in school, a rented flat in Ojodu, and what I thought was a reasonable life.

Then I made a decision that looked smart at the time.

Three friends and I decided to pool money and start a small import business. We'd bring in electronics — phones mostly — sell them quickly, split the profit. Clean. Simple. I put in my savings. I also took a personal loan to increase my stake. I trusted these men. We had been friends since NYSC.

You already know how this ends.

One of them disappeared with the money before the first shipment cleared customs. The other two said they had nothing to repay me with. No police case went anywhere. No recovery. Nothing. Just gone.

I was left holding a loan I had taken in my name alone, with no return to show for it.

That was the first crack.

Then the economy did what the economy does. My salary — which had not increased in three years — became worth less every month as prices rose around it. School fees went up. Rent went up. Transport went up. Everything went up except my income.

I started using one debt to cover another. I told myself it was temporary. That I'd stabilise soon.

Everyone says they're going to stabilise soon.

The debts multiplied. The calls started. The WhatsApp messages. The colleagues who stopped eating lunch with me because they knew I owed someone they knew. The shame of checking my balance every morning and immediately putting the phone face down.

My wife Ngozi is not a quiet woman. She is a caring woman — but she is not quiet. And when things got bad, the house became a different kind of place.

"Kuffey, the school sent another message."

"Kuffey, my mother needs something for the hospital."

"Kuffey, I've been wearing this same pair of shoes since Christmas."

She wasn't being cruel. She was scared. We were both scared. But fear in a marriage sounds different when it comes from two directions at once.

One night, after the children had gone to bed, Ngozi sat across from me at the kitchen table and said very quietly:

"I didn't marry suffering. I married you. But right now, I can't tell the difference."

That sentence broke something in me that has never fully healed.

My mother's elder sister — a woman I call Mama Chika — pulled me aside at a family gathering a few weeks later. She must have seen something in my face that I was trying to hide from everyone else.

She held my hand and said:

"My son. The problem is not that you fell. Every man falls. The problem is when you keep running in the same direction that made you fall. You must stop first. Assess. Then move."

I heard her. But I didn't know how. That's the part no one tells you — not the motivation to stop, but the actual mechanics of it.

The things I tried before I found the answer.

I am not proud of some of this. But I need you to see that I tried everything before I found what actually works.

Strict budget enforcement. I made a detailed budget. Colour-coded. I was very thorough. I lasted eleven days before an unexpected expense — a burst water pipe — erased the whole thing and I was back to scrambling. A budget doesn't work when the foundation underneath it is already cracked.

Family near-starvation. I reduced the food budget so aggressively that Ngozi started hiding bread under her pillow like we were in a refugee camp. My daughter asked me one evening why we never had "nice food" anymore. I had no answer. And the savings from eating less didn't even cover one month's interest. The sacrifice was real. The result was nothing.

Multiple jobs. I took on weekend freelance work. I helped a friend with his shop after hours. I hustled until 11pm on weekdays when I had to be back in the office by 7am. My health started breaking down. I was exhausted, irritable, and making mistakes at work that put my main income at risk. More income doesn't fix a leaking bucket.

Crowdfunding and peer grants. I asked my church group. I wrote appeals to old classmates. Some gave. Most disappeared. The ones who gave expected something I couldn't always deliver — gratitude performed in ways that cost me dignity. And it was never enough. You cannot borrow your way to stability.

Payday loans. I am not even going to spend long on this one. The interest rates on these things are a kind of financial violence. Every month I repaid, they took a slice that made next month harder. It was a trap dressed as help.

Loan consolidation. I managed to combine several debts into one single loan at a slightly lower rate. It felt like progress for exactly three months. Then a new emergency arrived — as they always do — and I had to take on new debt to cover it, and I was right back to having multiple debts again, except now with a consolidation loan on top.

Counselling. I saw a financial counsellor through my employer's wellness programme. He was kind. He gave me worksheets. He talked about mindset. I completed the worksheets. The mindset didn't pay the debt. I needed a system, not a therapist.

The encounter that changed things happened at a work-related event. A dinner. The kind where everyone is dressed well and pretending everything is fine. I was pretending harder than most.

I was seated next to a man I hadn't met before. His name was Elder Abadume. He was maybe fifty-five, grey at the temples, the kind of man who doesn't fidget. He had a stillness to him that I noticed immediately. Like nothing could shock him. Like he had already seen the worst version of everything and made peace with it.

We got to talking — the way men do at these events, slowly, guardedly, through conversation about work and the economy and the price of fuel.

And then, I don't know why, I said it. Maybe I was tired of performing. Maybe his calmness made me drop my guard. I told him, briefly, what was happening. The debt. The struggle. The failed attempts.

He didn't flinch. He didn't give me that look — the one that mixes pity with judgment. He just nodded slowly, like I was describing something he had heard before.

"There is nothing new under heaven," he said quietly. "No matter the calamity, the earth can handle it. And so can you. But not the way you're going about it."

He asked me something unexpected: "When you have a deep wound, what do you do first — stitch it or clean it?"

I said, clean it.

"Exactly," he said. "You are trying to stitch a wound that is still dirty inside. That is why it keeps getting infected. Every solution you've tried has been a stitch. None of them have been the cleaning."

He told me about a structured approach he had developed over many years — through his own financial near-collapse in the late 90s, through watching dozens of other men go through the same cycle, through reading, testing, and refining.

He called it simple. It is not quick — he was clear about that. But it is complete. It doesn't leave gaps. It cleans the wound properly before it stitches.

He said: "Most people want to sprint to 'debt-free.' But what they need first is to stop bleeding. Until you stop the bleeding, all the sprinting just makes it worse."

I'll be honest with you. When he walked me through it that night — over terrible small chops and warm Coke — I thought he was oversimplifying. It seemed too structured. Too clean. Too calm.

I was in chaos. Surely the solution had to be equally chaotic to match it?

But I was desperate. And I had nothing to lose by trying something new.

He gave me his number before we parted. I called him two days later and he walked me through the full system step by step.

Reveal. Stabilise. Recover. Protect.

Four stages. Each one builds on the last. Each one has a specific purpose. None of them asks you to be superhuman. None of them ignores the reality of living in Nigeria right now, with a family to feed and a salary that doesn't stretch.

The first stage — Reveal — required me to do something that felt humiliating. A complete, honest accounting of every naira in and out of my life. Not an estimate. Not a rough guess. Every single thing. I found things I hadn't wanted to see. Small leaks I'd been ignoring. Patterns I'd been blind to.

The second stage — Stabilise — was not about paying off debt. It was about stopping the bleeding first. Getting to a point where I was no longer creating new debt every month. It required a specific kind of budget — not a punishment budget, but a survival budget designed to last long enough to actually work.

Weeks passed. I will not pretend the early weeks were dramatic. They weren't. There was no miracle moment. There was just... less chaos. Slowly. Quietly. The daily financial panic that had become my constant companion started to ease — just slightly — like the first morning after a fever breaks.

Ngozi noticed first. She didn't say anything for a while. But one evening she made my favourite soup without me asking, and she was humming while she cooked. That was the real sign. She felt something had shifted.

Then, about six weeks in, she said it directly:

"Kuffey. Something is different. What are you doing?"

I showed her the system. She sat with it for a long time. Then she looked at me and said:

"Why didn't we have this from the beginning? This is just... sensible. This is just sense."

That was the moment I cried. Not from sadness. From something closer to relief so deep it hurt.

I later learned that two other men at that same dinner — a man named Biodun from Ibadan, and another called Emmanuel from Port Harcourt — had also spoken to Elder Abadume that evening and received parts of the same teaching.

Biodun told me three months later: "Brother, I am not going to exaggerate. I'm not debt-free yet. But for the first time in four years, I know exactly where I am. I know what I owe. I know what's coming. That alone — just knowing — changed everything. I sleep now. I actually sleep."

Emmanuel was more dramatic about it. He called me one afternoon almost shouting: "The Stabilise stage alone saved my marriage. My wife was packing bags, Kuffey. Packing bags. Now she's not packing bags."

These are not wealthy men. They are men like you and like me, trying to navigate a difficult country with dignity.

And this system works for men like us.

I started getting messages. From people who heard about my situation — or former situation — and wanted to know what I had done. First it was two people. Then five. Then too many to handle individually.

I tried explaining the system in voice notes. In long WhatsApp messages. On phone calls at midnight. But I kept leaving things out. I kept forgetting steps. And every person's situation had slightly different details that needed the full picture to address properly.

So I made a decision.

I sat down and documented everything — every insight Elder Abadume had shared with me, every stage, every worksheet, every lesson I had learned the hard way.

The result is not a summary. Not a motivational booklet. A full working document — built from real experience, written by a professional, designed for one purpose: to give you the same fighting chance I had.
